\begin{center}
{\large\gesperrt{Twenty-Second Chapter.}}

{\Large\textbf{Cayley's Development of the Modular Functions.}}

\rule{0.3\textwidth}{0.4pt}

\textbf{\S\,145. Limiting values for $s = 1$.}
\end{center}

In this chapter a function-theoretic application of the limit formula is to be given. Here, therefore, $\omega$ does not denote a quadratic irrational number, but a variable with positive imaginary part. The modular functions belong to the functions with \gesperrt{natural boundaries}, that is: as one approaches the boundary of convergence, the singular points lie everywhere dense upon that boundary, here therefore upon the axis of the real $\omega$, so that these functions cannot be continued analytically beyond this boundary. \gesperrt{Cayley} gave, in his last investigations, developments of the modular functions which are remarkable because they make evident the behaviour of the functions on approach to the boundary. The key to these developments is the limit formula § 141, (27)$^{1)}$.

If one puts in this formula:

\weq{1}{\begin{aligned}
&\mathop{\mathrm{Lim}}\limits_{s=1} \sum^{x,y} \frac{1}{(Ax^2+2Bxy+Cy^2)^s} - \frac{\pi}{(s-1)\sqrt m}\\
&= -\frac{2\pi\Gamma'(1)}{\sqrt m} + \frac{\pi}{\sqrt m}\log\frac{A}{4m} - \frac{2\pi}{\sqrt m}\log \eta(\omega_1),\ \eta(\omega_2),
\end{aligned}}
\[
\omega_1 = \frac{B+i\sqrt m}{A}, \quad \omega_2 = \frac{-B+i\sqrt m}{A},
\]

\weq{2}{\omega_1 = \alpha+\beta i, \quad \omega_2 = -\alpha+\beta i, \quad \beta > 0}
